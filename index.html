When complexity makes it hard to see what matters most.

An independent thinking partner for people and organisations navigating complex situations — when the path forward is uncertain, the decisions feel consequential and thinking it through alone is no longer enough.

Most difficult situations share one quality: the real challenge is harder to see than the visible symptoms. Things feel off, but the diagnosis isn't obvious. Effort is high, yet nothing is moving. The people are capable, but something in the way the work is organised is preventing progress.

I work with people to make sense of those situations — and then to work through them. Not by offering ready-made answers, but by thinking honestly together about what is actually happening, what it requires and what to do next.

When people reach out

The situations that bring us together

These conversations rarely begin with a clear question. More often, it is a sense that something important is not working — and that thinking it through alone is no longer enough.

01
Something feels wrong but the diagnosis isn't clear

There is a persistent sense that the organisation — or a major initiative — is not moving as it should. The reports look reasonable, people are working hard, but something beneath the surface isn't right and nobody has yet named it.

02
Decisions keep getting delayed or revisited

Forums exist, information is available and the right people are in the room — but decisions are slow, avoided or made and then undone. Whether this is a people problem, a structural problem or something else entirely remains unclear.

03
Everything feels equally urgent

Priorities compete without resolution. Resources are stretched across too many things that all claim to be critical, and there is no shared mechanism for making trade-offs explicit and sticking to them.

04
The leader has lost visibility of what is real

What arrives at the top — the data, the updates, the presentations — no longer reflects what is actually happening. The gap between the reported picture and the real one has quietly grown.

05
A transformation has stalled without an obvious reason

A major change programme was launched with genuine commitment. The intention was real. But somewhere between the plan and the present, momentum was lost and nobody is quite sure where or why.

06
The day-to-day is consuming what the future needs

Operational demands are consistently crowding out strategic thinking. The leader knows this cannot continue, but the immediate always seems to win over the important.

How we work together

Three things that are always true.

There is no fixed programme. But certain things are consistent across every engagement, regardless of how the work is shaped or how long it runs.

The real situation has to come first

Before anything else, we need to be working from the same account of what is actually happening. This sounds obvious, but in complex organisations, what reaches the top is rarely the complete picture. Reporting systems, cultural dynamics and the pressure to appear in control all work against honest visibility. The early work — sometimes the most important work — is establishing what is true, not what the data suggests or what people believe others want to hear.

Once that is in place, a great deal becomes possible. Decisions that seemed stuck often become straightforward. The right next step is usually visible once the actual situation is.

Structural problems need structural answers

Most of the situations that bring people to this kind of conversation have a structural dimension that is easy to miss. When decisions slow down, when accountability becomes diffuse, when priorities compete without resolution — the temptation is to look for individual explanations. Someone isn't leading strongly enough, or the team isn't aligned, or the strategy needs to be communicated better.

Sometimes that is true. But often the difficulty lies elsewhere: in how the work is organised, how decisions are authorised, how ownership is defined. Naming that gap clearly, rather than working around it, is usually where the real progress begins.

The goal is your independence, not a continuing engagement

Understanding what is happening is the beginning, not the end. The work continues through deciding what the situation requires, making the necessary changes and staying with it long enough that the progress is real. But the intention behind every engagement is the same: to leave the organisation more capable of handling the next difficulty on its own — not more reliant on an outside perspective.

This shapes everything about how the work is done. The aim is not to become indispensable. It is to become unnecessary.

Ways of working together

The form follows the situation

There is no standard format. Most collaborations begin with a conversation — about what the situation is, what it seems to require and whether working together makes sense. What happens after that depends on what we find.

Starting with the situation
Where most things begin
How it works

Most engagements begin with a focused period of looking honestly at what is actually happening — across the organisation, the programme or the particular challenge at hand. Not an audit and not a review. An independent account of what the situation really is, and what it seems to require.

This is often the most valuable part of the work. People frequently describe it as the first time they have had a clear, grounded account of something they have been living inside for months.

When it makes sense

When something important isn't working but the problem isn't clearly defined. When an independent perspective is needed before deciding on next steps. When internal effort hasn't produced the traction that was hoped for.

Sometimes this is the whole engagement. More often it leads into the next phase of work.

Working through it together
How it works

Once the situation is understood, the work shifts to deciding what it requires and doing it. This can take many forms — redesigning how decisions are made, resolving competing priorities, rebuilding governance structures, working through a transition or simply having a consistent thinking partner available through a consequential period.

The common thread is continuity: a partner who knows the context and stays engaged long enough for the work to actually land.

When it makes sense

When the challenge is ongoing rather than episodic. When an organisation is going through a significant transition and needs someone to think through the harder questions alongside the people carrying it. When the situation is well understood but moving through it requires sustained outside perspective.

Focused structural work
How it works

Sometimes the situation is well understood and the specific problem is clear: decision rights are undefined, accountability has fragmented, priorities are competing without resolution or a governance structure has stopped working. The engagement is focused, targeted and aimed at practical change rather than analysis.

The outcome is not a framework or a set of recommendations. It is a change in how the organisation actually operates — one it can own and sustain without ongoing support.

When it makes sense

When the problem is structural rather than strategic. When accountability is fragmented, priorities are unresolved or decisions consistently get made in the wrong place by the wrong people. When the organisation needs to change how it operates, not just how it thinks about what it is doing.

About

Why I work this way

For more than twenty years I worked inside complex, regulated organisations — in pharmaceutical manufacturing, engineering leadership, portfolio governance and large-scale programme management across international environments.

What I kept observing was that the most capable people, in the most capable organisations, could still find themselves unable to see clearly or move forward. Not because of a lack of intelligence or effort. Because of something structural: the way the work was organised, the way decisions were made, the way accountability had quietly dissolved or the way the real picture had been replaced, over time, by a more comfortable one.

That pattern — the gap between what organisations believe is happening and what is actually happening — became the central question of my career. Eventually it brought me here: working directly with the people navigating exactly those situations, as an independent outside partner rather than an insider.

What I bring is twenty years of pattern recognition across complex environments and a genuine commitment to working through the hard questions alongside the people carrying them. Previous organisations include EGIS Pharmaceuticals, GSK Biologicals, Teva Pharmaceuticals, EuroAPI and Richter Gedeon.

One thing I have learned, and keep relearning: the answer rarely comes from looking harder at the problem. More often it arrives when we step back and begin asking a better question.
